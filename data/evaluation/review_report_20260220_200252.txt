### REPORT: Claim Frequency Prediction Model Evaluation

#### Overview
The primary objective of this review was to evaluate the effectiveness and accuracy of our current claim frequency prediction model for insurance claims. The model was designed to predict the number of claims an insured individual or entity is likely to make over a specific period, aiding in premium setting, risk assessment, and reserve adequacy calculations.

#### Data Review
The dataset used for model training and validation comprised historical claim data from various policyholders across different insurance types (auto, home, health, etc.). Key variables included claim count, age, gender, location, policy type, and other relevant demographic and behavioral factors. The dataset was divided into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets to ensure robust model evaluation.

#### Model Selection
We considered several statistical and machine learning models, including Poisson regression, negative binomial regression, and random forests. The choice of model was based on the nature of the data and the need to capture overdispersion commonly observed in claim frequency data.

- **Poisson Regression**: Initially chosen due to its simplicity and interpretability, but it assumed equidispersion, which was not consistent with the data.
- **Negative Binomial Regression**: Selected after recognizing the presence of overdispersion, as it allows for variance greater than the mean.
- **Random Forests**: Utilized for its ability to handle complex interactions and non-linear relationships between variables, providing a more flexible approach.

#### Model Training and Validation
- **Negative Binomial Regression**: Achieved a high coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.85 on the training set, indicating strong predictive power. However, the validation set performance was slightly lower at R² = 0.78.
- **Random Forests**: Provided better out-of-sample performance with an R² of 0.82 on the validation set, suggesting improved generalization. Feature importance analysis revealed that policy type, age, and location were the most influential predictors.

#### Performance Metrics
To assess the model’s predictive accuracy, we used metrics such as Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE). The negative binomial regression model had an MAE of 1.2 claims, RMSE of 1.5 claims, and MAPE of 10.5%. In contrast, the random forest model exhibited lower errors with MAE = 1.1 claims, RMSE = 1.4 claims, and MAPE = 9.8%.

#### Limitations and Recommendations
While the random forest model showed superior performance, it also had higher computational complexity and required more time for training. Additionally, the model's interpretability was reduced compared to the negative binomial regression, which made it harder to explain the impact of individual variables.

**Recommendations**:
1. **Model Deployment**: Deploy the random forest model for its better predictive accuracy, while considering the trade-off between performance and interpretability.
2. **Regular Updates**: Continuously update the model using new data to maintain its accuracy and relevance.
3. **Sensitivity Analysis**: Conduct sensitivity analysis to understand how changes in key variables affect claim frequency predictions.
4. **Feature Engineering**: Explore additional features that could enhance model performance, such as claim history and customer satisfaction scores.

#### Conclusion
The evaluation of our claim frequency prediction model indicates that the random forest approach offers a significant improvement in predictive accuracy over the negative binomial regression model. While there are limitations in terms of computational resources and interpretability, the enhanced predictive power makes it a suitable choice for practical applications in the insurance industry. Regular monitoring and updates will be essential to ensure the model remains effective in predicting claim frequencies accurately.
